Metal roofs and [solar panels](/how-long-until-solar-panels-pay-for-themselves/) are genuinely well-matched, and the installation is straightforward once you understand which attachment method your roof actually needs. The confusion usually comes from contractors who specialize in asphalt shingles trying to retrofit their standard workflow onto a standing seam or corrugated metal surface. That's where the problems start.

The good news: metal roofs typically last 40 to 70 years, which means you won't have to tear off panels mid-system-life to reroof the way you would with composition shingles. That alone makes metal one of the better substrates for solar.

## The Two Roof Types That Change Everything

Not all metal roofs are the same, and the installation approach splits almost entirely based on whether you have a standing seam or an exposed-fastener profile.

**Standing seam** means the fasteners that hold the metal panels to the deck are hidden beneath raised ribs that run vertically up the roof. The seam itself locks panels together. This is the gold standard for solar because you can use seam clamps from manufacturers like S-5! that grip those raised ribs mechanically, with no holes drilled through the metal. Zero penetrations. Your roof warranty stays intact (provided you use clamps the manufacturer approves), and water infiltration risk is essentially eliminated at the attachment points.

**Exposed-fastener profiles** (corrugated, R-panel, 5-V crimp) are more common on older homes and agricultural buildings. The screws that hold the metal are visible. Solar mounts here require drilling, and you need to hit the structural members beneath, not just the metal skin. Each penetration needs a butyl-pad flashing foot and a compatible sealant. It's more involved, but it's done routinely.

There's a third category worth mentioning: stone-coated steel tiles and aluminum shingles. These mimic traditional roofing and generally require a tile-hook attachment similar to what you'd use on concrete tile. If you have one of these, make sure your installer has actually done it before on that specific profile.

## Attachment Hardware and What to Ask For

The clamp market for standing seam is well-developed. S-5! makes the most widely specified line and publishes load testing data for virtually every standing seam profile. Their PVKIT system is common for solar rail mounting, and load tables are publicly available on their site so you can verify your engineer's assumptions. SolarEdge, Unirac, and IronRidge all publish rail compatibility with S-5! clamps, so mixing brands isn't a guessing game.

For exposed-fastener roofs, the mount needs to be a "top-hat" style foot with a butyl tape backing that compresses under the mounting screw to form a seal. SnapNrack and Unirac both make metal-roof-specific products for this. The screw itself should be a #14 or #12 self-drilling with a neoprene washer, going into a rafter or purlin beneath, not floating in the metal panel.

One thing that often gets glossed over: on metal roofs with a thermal gap or over open framing (common in agricultural and some residential standing seam applications), the attachment must bridge to solid wood or steel below. If your installer doesn't mention locating rafters or purlins before marking layout, ask the question directly.

## Grounding and Electrical Considerations

Metal roofs change the grounding picture in a useful way. Under NEC Article 690 and Article 250, a metal roof that's properly bonded to the structure can serve as part of the equipment grounding conductor path for the racking system. This isn't a shortcut that every AHJ (authority having jurisdiction) will approve without documentation, but it's a legitimate path supported by code when done correctly. Your installer [should know](/solaredge-nexis-just-hit-the-us-market-what-homeowners-should-know/) whether the local inspector requires a separate grounding conductor run regardless.

One real consideration: galvanic corrosion. Aluminum racking on a steel roof without proper isolation can corrode at the contact points over years. Stainless hardware and neoprene or EPDM isolation pads between dissimilar metals matter here. It's not a dramatic failure mode, but it's the kind of thing that shows up on a 25-year system inspection if someone cut corners on year one.

## Sizing and Production on Metal Roofs

Metal roofs don't change the fundamental sizing math, but a few things are worth flagging.

A common misconception is that metal roofs run hotter and therefore hurt panel production. The roof surface temperature does run high, but what matters is the temperature of the panels themselves, not the roof. Standard racking holds panels 3 to 4 inches off the surface, allowing airflow. Research on building-integrated and racking-mounted PV consistently shows that panel temperature depends much more on that air gap and local ambient conditions than on roof material below.

What metal roofs *can* affect is the acoustic environment during design-load wind events and hail. This isn't a production issue, but if you're in a hail-prone area, panel placement to avoid the most exposed ridgelines is worth discussing with your installer.

**Example:** A 1,500 sq. ft. usable south-facing metal roof at 25° pitch in Denver (average 5.5 peak sun hours/day). Assuming standard 400W panels at 20% efficiency and roughly 70% of roof area available for array layout, you could fit approximately 24 to 28 panels (9.6 to 11.2 kW). At 80% system efficiency (accounting for inverter losses and wiring), that's roughly 42 to 49 kWh/day on average, or 15,000 to 18,000 kWh/year. These numbers assume no shading and standard temperature derating. Run them through NREL's PVWatts Calculator (free, at pvwatts.nrel.gov) with your actual address and tilt for a site-specific estimate.

## Permits, HOAs, and Roof Warranties

Permits work the same as any residential solar project: you'll need a building permit and often a separate electrical permit. The structural piece gets more scrutiny on metal roofs because the AHJ wants to see that racking loads are transferred to structure, not just to the metal skin. Have your installer provide a stamped letter or engineering calc showing attachment point loads and member sizing. This isn't optional in most jurisdictions, and skipping it creates problems at inspection.

HOAs are a separate issue. If you're in an HOA, check your state's solar access laws before assuming the HOA can block you. Most states now have statutes limiting HOA authority to restrict solar, though they vary considerably in scope. The HOA can often still regulate aesthetics like panel color and placement visibility from the street.

On roof warranties: this is the one [most homeowners](/the-july-4-solar-deadline-most-homeowners-dont-know-about/) miss. Metal roof manufacturers including Englert, MBCI, and McElroy Metal all publish approved attachment lists. If your installer uses a clamp or penetration method not on that approved list, some manufacturers will void the roof warranty even on a standing seam. Ask for the written warranty language before work starts, not after.
