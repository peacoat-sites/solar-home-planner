Walmart started selling home battery subscriptions in stores this November, and that's a bigger shift than it might look at first glance. On October 30, 2026, Walmart announced a strategic investment in Base Power, a Texas-based home battery company, with in-store enrollment rolling out at participating Walmart and Sam's Club locations immediately. You can now sign up for a home battery system the same trip you buy motor oil and paper towels. That's not a gimmick. That's a distribution channel with 4,600 U.S. stores reaching roughly 90% of the American population.

The timing isn't accidental. The federal Section 25D residential clean energy tax credit, which gave homeowners a 30% credit on the purchase of a [battery storage](/how-much-battery-storage-for-off-grid-home/) system, expired after December 31, 2025. If you bought and owned your own battery in 2024, you got that credit. If you're buying in 2026, you don't. That changes the math on owning versus leasing a battery in a meaningful way, and Base Power's subscription model is positioned to fill exactly that gap.

Base Power's pitch is straightforward: they install, own, and maintain the battery. You pay a modest upfront installation fee (20% off if you enroll through Walmart or Sam's Club) plus an estimated monthly lease of roughly $19 to $29. You're not financing a $10,000 to $15,000 purchase. You're paying for access to [backup power](/solar-backup-power-during-outage/) and grid services on a subscription basis, closer to how you pay for internet than how you pay for a water heater.

## What Base Power's Model Actually Is

Base Power is not a [solar company](/solar-company-certifications-nabcep/). It doesn't sell panels. It installs battery storage, connects it to your home, and then uses that battery to participate in grid services, primarily in Texas's ERCOT market. Members get backup power during outages and may see bill credits depending on how Base Power dispatches the battery. The company serves more than 35,000 members in Texas, with over 20,000 batteries already installed as of its Walmart announcement.

The company raised a $1 billion Series D in August 2026 at a $13 billion post-money valuation and launched domestic manufacturing of its Base Core battery at an Austin factory. That production capacity is what makes the 100 MW per month installation target credible rather than just marketing. At that pace, they'd be deploying gigawatt-hours of storage per year, which puts them in serious utility-scale territory even though each individual installation is residential.

## Own vs. Subscribe: The Numbers After the Tax Credit Expired

This is where the policy change really bites. Before 2026, a homeowner buying a 10 kWh battery at, say, $10,000 all-in could claim $3,000 back on their federal taxes. That brought the effective cost to $7,000. That option is gone for 2026 purchases. The Section 25D credit expired, and as of this writing, Congress has not reinstated it.

Here's a rough side-by-side so you can see where the subscription model sits:

| Factor | Owned Battery (2026) | Base Power Subscription |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cost | $8,000–$15,000+ installed | Reduced installation fee (20% off with Walmart/Sam's Club) |
| Federal tax credit | None (expired after 2025) | N/A, you don't own the asset |
| Monthly cost | $0 (after payoff) | ~$19–$29/month |
| Who owns the hardware | You | Base Power |
| Maintenance responsibility | You (or warranty) | Base Power |
| Equity / resale value | Yes | No |
| Grid services revenue | Depends on your setup | Base Power dispatches, may share credits |

The subscription wins on upfront cost and simplicity. The ownership path wins if you hold the asset long enough, can claim future incentives if the credit is reinstated, and want equity in your home energy system. Neither is universally better. It depends on how long you stay in your home, what your state offers in additional incentives, and how much you value the convenience of not being responsible for maintenance.

## What the Walmart Distribution Channel Changes

Retail distribution for home energy products isn't new. You can buy a Generac portable generator at Home Depot. What's different here is the subscription enrollment happening at point of sale, combined with Walmart's reach into lower- and middle-income households that might not have been in a solar installer's marketing funnel before.

Base Power's existing Texas footprint shows the model works at scale. Getting 20,000 batteries installed with a subscription service, in a single state, before a Walmart partnership, is real traction. The Walmart deal is an accelerant, not a proof of concept. The 20% discount for Walmart and Sam's Club enrollees is a concrete incentive, not a vague "member benefit," and it lowers the barrier for customers who might otherwise balk at any upfront cost at all.

There's also a credibility signal here. Homeowners who are skeptical of door-to-door solar salespeople, and there are good reasons to be skeptical given the industry's history with high-pressure tactics, may be more comfortable with a transaction that starts inside a store they already trust.

## Red Flags and Real Limitations to Check Before You Sign

Read the subscription contract carefully. The key questions are: What's the minimum term? What are the buyout terms if you want to end the subscription early or if you sell your home? Who is responsible if the battery causes property damage? What happens if Base Power dispatches the battery in ways that leave you without backup power during an outage?

Base Power's current market is Texas, where ERCOT's grid structure makes grid services revenue straightforward. Expansion outside Texas means different grid operators, different rules, and potentially different economics for the subscriber. If you're not in Texas right now, you're waiting for the rollout, and the terms you're eventually offered may differ from what current Texas members have.

The subscription model also means you're building no equity. If you move in four years, that battery stays with the house or the contract transfers or terminates. Compare that to an owned battery, which can be listed as a home feature and may add resale value, though that value is genuinely hard to quantify in most markets.

Base Power's Walmart deal marks the moment home battery storage stopped being a product for early adopters with cash to spend and started being a service you pick up between the grocery and hardware aisles. With the federal tax credit gone for owned systems, the low-upfront subscription path looks more competitive than it did even a year ago. Whether that's the right call for your home depends on your time horizon, your state's incentive picture, and what a subscription contract actually says once you read the fine print.
