Something shifted this fall. Quietly, without much fanfare in the mainstream press, the number of U.S. states where renters can legally plug a solar panel into a standard outlet and offset their electricity bill hit ten. California's SB 868, the Plug Into the Sun Act, was signed by Governor Newsom on September 30, 2026. New Jersey's Garden State [Balcony Solar](/apsystems-ez1-lv-plug-in-balcony-solar-arrives-in-the-us/) Act takes effect March 1, 2027. Colorado's HB26-1007 went live August 12. If you're a renter, a condo owner, or an apartment dweller who assumed solar was permanently off the table because you don't own a roof, the legal picture has changed faster than most people realize.

This isn't a niche hobbyist thing anymore. Plug-in solar, sometimes called balcony solar after the European model that's been common in Germany and Austria for years, refers to small panel systems, typically one or two panels, that connect to your home's electrical grid through a standard outlet or a dedicated receptacle. The inverter pushes power onto your internal circuit, your meter slows down, and your bill drops. Simple concept. The holdup in the U.S. has always been legal and regulatory, not technical. That holdup is now dissolving state by state, and a panel discussion at pv magazine USA Week in November 2026 called it an accelerating regulatory shift actively moving plug-in solar into the American mainstream.

New York's SUNNY Act has already passed the legislature and is waiting on Governor Hochul's signature before year-end. At the rate state houses have been moving, the ten-state count could grow before spring.

## Which States Are In, and What the Laws Actually Say

The ten states with signed legislation are Utah, Maine, Virginia, Maryland, Colorado, Connecticut, New Hampshire, Vermont, New Jersey, and California. That's a geographic spread from New England to the Mountain West to the Pacific, and the specific rules vary enough that you need to look at your state's law, not just assume they're identical.

| State | Key Law | Wattage Cap | Landlord/HOA Protection | Effective Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Colorado | HB26-1007 | 1,920 W | Prohibited from blocking (Jan 1, 2027) | Aug 12, 2026 |
| New Jersey | Garden State Balcony Solar Act | Not specified | 14-day written notice required | Mar 1, 2027 |
| California | SB 868 (Plug Into the Sun Act) | Not specified | Landlords may not prohibit | Jan 1, 2027 |
| Vermont | State plug-in solar bill | Varies | Protections included | 2026 |
| New Hampshire | State plug-in solar bill | Varies | Protections included | 2026 |

Colorado's law is the most tenant-friendly on wattage. At 1,920 watts, you could run two standard 400-watt panels with a quality microinverter and still have headroom. That's enough to put a real dent in a typical apartment electricity bill. Most other state laws and the general U.S. regulatory framework have historically pointed toward 600 watts as a soft ceiling, so Colorado is an outlier worth noting if you're shopping systems before deciding whether to relocate.

The effective dates matter. California's SB 868 doesn't kick in until January 1, 2027. If your landlord in California tells you no [right now](/your-solar-installer-went-bankrupt-what-to-do-right-now/), they're technically still within their rights until that date. New Jersey's March 1, 2027 effective date gives landlords and property managers a runway to update lease language. Know your state's date before you order hardware.

## The UL 3700 Certification Change You Should Actually Care About

For years, one of the practical problems with plug-in solar in the U.S., even where it wasn't explicitly illegal, was that no product had full UL 3700 certification. That's the safety standard specifically written for plug-in inverter systems. Without it, your homeowner's insurance could deny a claim, your utility could object, and frankly, you were on shaky ground.

That changed in July 2026. The APsystems EZ1 microinverter and a certified Hoymiles microinverter both earned full UL 3700 certification, according to reporting tracked through solar.com's October 2026 plug-in solar guide. These aren't obscure brands. APsystems and Hoymiles are both established microinverter manufacturers with real market share in residential solar. Certified products from known manufacturers means retail availability through legitimate channels, not grey-market imports, and it means your installer or electrician has a code-compliant path to follow.

If you're buying a plug-in [solar kit](/diy-solar-kit-recommendations/), UL 3700 certification on the inverter should be a non-negotiable. Any seller who can't tell you the certification status of their inverter is a red flag.

## What Renters Can and Can't Do Right Now

The laws protect your right to install. They don't necessarily sort out every landlord conversation for you. New Jersey's approach is worth understanding because it's one of the clearest written tenant protections: you give 14 days' written notice, and that's legally sufficient. You don't need permission. You need to give notice. That's a meaningful distinction.

What you're generally installing is small. A typical setup is one or two panels, maybe 400 to 800 watts total, mounted on a balcony railing or laid flat on a patio, connected through a dedicated plug or a standard 20-amp outlet. You're not drilling through the roof. You're not modifying the electrical panel. The installation is designed to be removable when you move out, which is part of why the laws treat it differently from a full rooftop system.

A few practical things to sort out before you buy anything. First, check whether your balcony faces the right direction. South-facing is best in the northern hemisphere, southeast and southwest are workable. A north-facing balcony in a shaded courtyard is going to give you disappointing production numbers regardless of what the law says. Second, check your lease for any blanket language about alterations. Even in protected states, knowing what your lease says before you send that written notice is smarter than triggering a dispute. Third, ask your utility about net metering or billing credit for exported power. The laws legalize installation. They don't automatically guarantee your utility will credit you for any surplus power your system feeds back.

## HOAs Are a Separate Fight

Homeowners associations are explicitly covered in Colorado's law, which prohibits them from blocking plug-in solar starting January 1, 2027. Other state laws vary on how clearly they address HOAs versus just landlords. If you're in a condo with an HOA, don't assume the state law automatically overrides your association's rules until you've read the specific language of your state's legislation. Pluginsolarguide.com's November 2026 state-by-state breakdown is a reasonable starting point for checking your specific state's HOA provisions.

If your HOA sends a violation notice after your state's effective date and your state's law includes HOA protections, that notice likely has no legal standing. Document everything in writing and keep copies of the law citation handy.

Ten states in one year is a real shift. The products are certified. The legal framework is building. If you've been waiting for plug-in solar to become a legitimate, protected option for renters, that moment is arriving, state by state, right now.
