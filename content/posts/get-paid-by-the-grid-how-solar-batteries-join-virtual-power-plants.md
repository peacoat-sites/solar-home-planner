The 30% [federal tax credit](/solar-payback-period-without-the-federal-tax-credit/) that made residential solar math easy is gone. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act let Section 25D expire on December 31, 2025, and there's no replacement on the table for homeowners who buy their systems outright. That's a real hit: on a $20,000 battery-plus-solar install, you just lost $6,000. But something else has been filling that gap quietly, and if you have a home battery or are shopping for one right now, it's worth understanding before you sign anything.

Virtual power plants, VPPs, are programs where a utility or aggregator coordinates thousands of [home batteries](/home-batteries-are-booming-while-solar-stalls-what-it-means-for-you/) to discharge simultaneously during peak grid stress events, typically hot summer afternoons or cold snaps when demand spikes. Your battery does what it was going to do anyway, back up your house, except that the utility can call on that capacity and pay you for it. Sunrun reported in July 2026 that its distributed fleet had crossed 80,000 participating households with more than 110,000 batteries, representing up to 425 MW of dispatchable peak capacity. That's a mid-size power plant built out of garage walls and laundry rooms. The scale matters because it signals that utilities are treating residential storage as real infrastructure, not a pilot program.

PV Magazine reported on November 3, 2026 that declining [net metering](/net-metering-changes-threatening-solar-roi/) export rates and grid capacity constraints are pushing installers nationwide toward "storage-first" sales models. The logic is straightforward: if the grid won't pay much for your exported electrons anymore, the case for a battery attached to that solar array just got stronger, and VPP enrollment is part of that stronger case.

## What VPPs Actually Pay, and Where

The numbers vary enormously by program, utility, and state. California is currently the most developed market, partly because CPUC extended the Emergency Load Reduction Program battery component through 2029 in a decision issued October 29, 2026. That regulatory certainty has given both utilities and aggregators room to design real payment structures.

Here's what a 13.5 kWh battery (the size of a single Tesla Powerwall 3) earns in the best California programs right now:

| Program | Upfront Enrollment Payment | Annual Ongoing Payment |
|---|---|---|
| SMUD (Sacramento) | Up to $4,725 | Up to $440/year |
| San Diego Community Power | Up to $972 | Up to $50/year |
| Typical statewide range | $972–$4,725 | $50–$440/year |

Outside California, programs exist but are less mature. Texas, Hawaii, and several Northeast states have active aggregator programs. If you're not in California, ask any installer quoting you a battery specifically which VPP or demand response program they can enroll you in, and get the payment structure in writing before you sign.

## The Tax Credit Workaround Nobody Is Advertising Enough

Here's where the ownership structure of your system gets genuinely important. If you buy your solar-plus-battery system outright or with a loan, the federal Section 25D credit is gone. Full stop. But if you go with a lease or power purchase agreement, the installer owns the hardware, and they can claim the Section 48E commercial investment tax credit, which covers standalone storage and solar-plus-storage. That credit is currently 30% of system cost for projects placed in service before the phase-down kicks in.

Some installers are passing a portion of that value back to customers through lower monthly payments or reduced buyout prices. Some are not. This isn't charity; it's competition. If you're getting quotes and one installer is offering a significantly lower PPA rate than others, the 48E credit is probably part of why. Ask directly: "Are you claiming the Section 48E credit on this system, and how does that affect my rate?" If they look confused, that's information.

The tradeoff is real. You don't own the battery, so VPP enrollment and payments flow through the installer. Read the lease or PPA carefully to see who keeps VPP revenue. Some contracts split it; some don't.

## Sizing for VPP Participation, Not Just Backup

Most VPP programs have minimum capacity requirements. Many require at least one battery with 10 kWh or more of usable storage, and some aggregators prefer 20 kWh or above because larger systems give the grid more reliable capacity. If you're sizing a battery primarily for overnight backup, you might land at 10–13.5 kWh and still qualify. If you're in a high-rate area and want to run whole-home backup through a multi-day outage, you're probably looking at two battery modules anyway, and that larger system makes you a more attractive VPP participant.

Before you finalize a size, it helps to run the numbers on your actual load. A [battery bank sizing calculator](/solar-battery-calculator/) can walk you through how much storage you need to cover your overnight consumption, so you're not undersizing for backup or massively oversizing for a VPP payout that doesn't justify the extra hardware cost.

One thing to check: VPP programs typically require your battery to have a specific state of charge before a dispatch event. SMUD's program, for example, requires the battery to be at or above a minimum charge threshold before they can call on it. That means your battery management settings matter. An installer who enrolls you and then doesn't configure the charge floor correctly is leaving money on the table, yours.

## Contractor Red Flags Specific to This Market

Residential battery storage installations hit record levels in early 2026, according to industry reports. High demand means the market attracted a lot of new entrants, some of whom know the hardware but not the VPP program details.

Watch for these specific problems. First, any installer claiming you'll earn a fixed guaranteed annual VPP payment without showing you the specific program contract. These are variable, dispatch-dependent payments, not annuities. Second, installers who can't name the specific aggregator or utility program they're enrolling you in. "We'll handle it" is not an answer. Get the program name, the administrator, and the payment structure. Third, quotes that include battery storage but don't mention VPP enrollment at all. In a post-25D market, that omission should prompt questions.

Also check permit scope. Battery storage systems require their own permits separate from solar in most jurisdictions, and they need to meet NEC 706 and your local fire marshal's requirements for placement and ventilation. An installer who is pulling a solar permit and treating the battery as an add-on without separate inspection is cutting a corner that could affect both your insurance and your ability to participate in utility programs that require compliant installations.

The financial case for home batteries in 2026 is genuinely different than it was two years ago: less federal subsidy, more utility revenue, and a faster-evolving program landscape. The homeowners who come out ahead will be the ones who treat VPP enrollment as a deliverable they're buying, not a bonus they might get.
