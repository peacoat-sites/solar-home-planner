[Solar panels](/solar-panels-for-low-income-homeowners-programs/) don't stop working in winter. That's the misconception worth clearing up first. Cold temperatures actually improve photovoltaic efficiency, and a snow-covered roof is only a temporary setback, not a reason to avoid solar. The real winter variables are daylight hours and panel angle, and those are things you can plan around before the first freeze.

What catches homeowners off guard isn't the cold itself. It's discovering that their system was sized for summer peaks and barely covers their December bill when days are short and they're running electric heat. That's a planning problem, not a physics problem.

## Why Cold Actually Helps (Up to a Point)

Photovoltaic cells are semiconductors. Like most semiconductors, they're more efficient at lower temperatures. The standard test condition for solar panels is 77°F (25°C), and manufacturers specify a temperature coefficient, typically somewhere around -0.3% to -0.5% per degree Celsius above that baseline. Run the math in reverse: a panel operating at 32°F (0°C) is about 12-25% more efficient than the same panel baking at 95°F (35°C) in July.

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) has documented this relationship extensively in their PVWatts modeling tool, which lets you run location-specific production estimates by month. If you haven't used PVWatts to check your winter numbers, you're guessing.

The catch, of course, is that cold days in December are also short. Gaining 15% from cool temperatures doesn't help much if you've got 8 hours of daylight instead of 15. Net winter production still drops in most of the northern U.S. and Canada, just not for the reason most people assume.

## Snow: The Real Story

Thin dustings usually melt or blow off within hours. A heavy wet snowfall is another matter, and yes, a few inches of snow blocking your panels will cut output dramatically, often to near zero. But a few honest points:

Panels on a roof angled at 30 degrees or steeper shed snow faster than you might expect. The glass surface is slick, and panels generate a small amount of heat during operation that accelerates melting at the edges. Most moderate snowfalls clear within one to three days without any intervention.

What you should NOT do is climb on an icy roof with a ladder. It's not worth it. If you have a ground-mounted system at accessible height, a soft foam roof rake or a purpose-made [solar panel cleaning](/solar-panel-cleaning-how-often-needed/) brush with an extendable handle can clear snow safely from the ground. The Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA) has flagged panel damage from improper snow removal as a surprisingly common warranty issue, usually because someone used a metal shovel.

One more thing: partial shading from snow is worse than total shading in many [string inverter](/microinverter-vs-string-inverter-comparison/) setups. If one panel in a string is half-covered and generating a reduced voltage, it can drag down the entire string's output. This is a strong argument for microinverters or DC optimizers in snowy climates, since each panel operates independently.

## Tilt Angle in Winter Months

Summer sun tracks high in the sky. Winter sun stays low on the horizon. The optimal tilt angle for capturing maximum annual energy is roughly equal to your latitude, but the optimal tilt for winter specifically is your latitude plus 15 degrees or so.

**Example:** A home at 40° latitude (roughly Denver, Indianapolis, or Philadelphia) with panels fixed at 40° tilt will perform reasonably well year-round. But a neighbor with the same roof pitched at only 20° will see noticeably worse performance in December and January, when the sun angle is around 25-30° above the horizon and a flatter panel catches less direct irradiance. The difference in December production between a 20° and 40° tilt at 40° latitude can run 10-15%, which compounds with shorter days.

If you have a flat roof or a very low-pitch installation, ask your installer about adjustable racking. Some commercial-grade adjustable mounts work on residential installations. They're more expensive and add wind load considerations, but in a cold climate with significant winter heating loads, the extra production can pencil out.

## Sizing for Winter: The Calculation Most Installers Skip

Here's what a lot of solar proposals don't show you clearly: system production by month, not just annual totals. An installer will often lead with "this system produces 10,000 kWh annually," but if 3,000 of those kWh come in June and July, and you're drawing 1,200 kWh in January to heat your home, you've got a mismatch.

**Example:** Assume a 10 kW system in Minneapolis at 40° tilt. Using NREL's PVWatts with Minneapolis climate data, a rough monthly output might look like this:

| Month | Estimated Output (kWh) | Average Daily Hours of Peak Sun |
|-------|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| January | 500–650 | 2.5–3.0 |
| February | 650–850 | 3.0–3.5 |
| March | 950–1,150 | 4.0–4.5 |
| April | 1,100–1,300 | 4.5–5.0 |
| May | 1,300–1,500 | 5.0–5.5 |
| June | 1,350–1,550 | 5.5–6.0 |
| July | 1,400–1,600 | 5.5–6.0 |
| August | 1,250–1,450 | 5.0–5.5 |
| September | 1,000–1,200 | 4.0–4.5 |
| October | 750–950 | 3.5–4.0 |
| November | 500–650 | 2.5–3.0 |
| December | 400–550 | 2.0–2.5 |

These are approximations based on PVWatts modeling assumptions, not guaranteed figures. Run your own address in PVWatts for real numbers. The point is that December output can be less than a third of July's. If you're on net metering, summer credits can offset winter bills, but only if your utility's program allows annual rollover. Some don't.

If you're battery-dependent or off-grid, sizing for January is non-negotiable. You cannot design for average performance and expect to stay powered through the worst week of the year.

## Maintenance Checklist Before Winter

Panels themselves are sealed units. There's not much to do internally. But a few things matter:

Check your monitoring app now. Set a production baseline in September so you have a reference point. If January output drops 60% below that baseline and there's no snow on the panels, something is wrong with your system, not just the weather.

Trim any deciduous trees that cast shadows between November and February. Winter shadows are longer and earlier in the afternoon than summer shadows from the same trees. What was a minor issue in July can become serious shading by December.

Clean your panels before the first freeze if they haven't been cleaned recently. Dust, pollen, and bird droppings baked on over summer can reduce output 5-7% on their own. A basic [solar panel cleaning kit](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=solar+panel+cleaning+kit&tag=contentportfo-20) with a soft brush and squeegee is enough for most residential installations (the site may earn a commission on purchases through this link).

If you have a [home energy monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=home+energy+monitor&tag=contentportfo-20) like an Emporia Vue or a Sense unit, winter is when it earns its keep. Watching your actual consumption versus production in real time helps you adjust usage patterns and catch system underperformance before you're staring at a large January utility bill (affiliate link).
