Most solar panels don't need replacing when you think they do. That's the honest answer. The industry standard warranty is 25 years, and most panels continue producing meaningful power well past that mark, just at a reduced output. The real question isn't "how old are my panels?" but "how much power are they actually producing compared to what they should be?"

Degradation happens in every panel, every year. The typical rate is around 0.5% per year, though some budget panels degrade faster. After 25 years at that rate, you're looking at roughly 88% of original output, not great, not catastrophic. Whether that's acceptable depends on your load, your battery setup if you have one, and what newer panels would cost you in comparison.

## Signs Your Panels May Actually Be Failing

Physical damage is the clearest trigger. Cracked glass, delamination (where the layers of the panel visually separate or bubble), and severe discoloration (beyond light yellowing, which is normal) all compromise performance and can create safety hazards. Delaminated panels can trap moisture, which leads to faster corrosion of the cell interconnects and, in some cases, ground faults.

Hotspots are worth calling out specifically. A hotspot is a localized area on a cell that gets dramatically hotter than the surrounding panel, usually because one cell is shaded or defective and the others are driving current through it. You can't see hotspots with the naked eye, but a thermal imaging inspection (many solar companies offer these; some energy auditors do too) will show them clearly. A single bad cell can drag down the entire string in a standard series-wired system.

Persistent underperformance is trickier. Before you blame the panels, check everything else. Inverter firmware, wiring connections, shading from new tree growth, and dirt accumulation all affect production. A good [home energy monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?tag=contentportfo-20&k=home+energy+monitor) can help you track real-time production and spot when output diverges from your historical baseline or the system's modeled performance.

## The 20% Rule (and When It Breaks Down)

A panel that's producing less than 80% of its rated output is generally considered to have degraded beyond acceptable limits. Most panel warranties actually use this threshold: if output drops below 80% of nameplate wattage within the warranty period, the manufacturer owes you a replacement or compensation.

That 80% floor matters, but apply it carefully. Nameplate wattage is measured under Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1000 W/m² of irradiance, 25°C cell temperature, air mass 1.5. Real-world conditions almost never match STC, so you can't just compare your meter reading on a partly cloudy afternoon to the nameplate. You need either a sunny-day test with a clamp meter, or better, production data compared against a solar irradiance tool like PVWatts (free from NREL at pvwatts.nrel.gov). PVWatts will show you what your system should produce given your location, tilt, azimuth, and panel specs, a consistent 20%+ shortfall from that model is a real red flag.

**Example:** Assume a 10-panel system, each panel rated 350W, installed 15 years ago. Modeled annual production via PVWatts: 14,200 kWh. Actual production last year from the utility or monitoring app: 10,900 kWh. That's 23% below model. At 0.5%/year degradation over 15 years, expected output would be about 92.5% of original, so modeled production after degradation should be roughly 13,135 kWh. You're at 10,900 kWh, which is roughly 83% of that degradation-adjusted figure. That gap, almost 17% below what even a degraded system should produce, means something beyond normal aging is happening. At that point you investigate before you replace.

EnergySage's market data consistently shows significant regional variation in installation costs, and the federal Investment [Tax Credit](/going-solar-without-the-30-tax-credit-what-it-actually-costs-now/) (ITC) currently at 30% for residential systems can apply to a qualifying replacement installation, which changes the math considerably.

## Don't Replace the Panels When It's Really the Inverter

This deserves its own section because it's one of the most common and expensive mistakes homeowners make. String inverters typically last 10-15 years. Microinverters and DC optimizers (like those from Enphase or SolarEdge) are designed to last longer, but they still fail. When an inverter or optimizer fails, it looks exactly like panel degradation from the outside: production drops, monitoring shows underperformance, and a less-than-thorough installer might suggest panel replacement.

Always isolate the inverter from the diagnosis. If you have a string inverter and the whole system is underperforming, start there. If you have microinverters and only certain panels are underperforming, pull the monitoring data at the panel level (Enphase's Enlighten app, for example, shows per-panel production). A microinverter replacement runs a few hundred dollars. Replacing panels you didn't need to replace could cost thousands.

## Replacement vs. Adding New Panels

One option people overlook: sometimes the right answer isn't replacing old panels but adding new ones if you have roof space. If your old system is producing adequately but you've added an EV or increased your load, new panels can be added to a separate string. Mixing old and new panels on the same string is a bad idea (mismatched IV curves reduce overall output), but a separate string with its own inverter or additional microinverters is clean and legal in most jurisdictions.

Adding panels also avoids the permitting complexity of a full system tear-down and reinstall. Any change to a permitted solar system typically requires a new permit and inspection. The [U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner solar guide](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has a solid overview of what the interconnection and permitting process looks like, which is worth reviewing before you sign anything.
