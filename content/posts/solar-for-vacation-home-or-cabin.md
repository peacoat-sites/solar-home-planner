Off-[grid solar](/off-grid-solar-system-cost-breakdown/) for a vacation home or cabin is genuinely different from a grid-tied rooftop system, and most of the advice online glosses over that distinction. If your cabin has no utility connection, you're not supplementing the grid, you're replacing it entirely. That changes everything about how you size the system, what equipment you buy, and what happens when the math is wrong.

## Off-Grid vs. Grid-Tied: Choose the Right Architecture First

A cabin with utility service available can run a standard grid-tied system, claim net metering credits, and use the grid as a free battery. That's the easy case. The harder, more common case is a property where utility power costs $30,000–$80,000 or more to extend from the nearest transformer. At that point, off-grid solar with [battery storage](/how-much-battery-storage-for-off-grid-home/) is often cheaper over a 20-year horizon, not just philosophically appealing.

Hybrid systems, sometimes called grid-tied with battery backup, exist for cabins that have utility service but suffer frequent outages. These use inverter-chargers like the Schneider Electric XW+ or the SMA Sunny Island to maintain a [critical loads panel](/critical-loads-panel-solar-backup/) even when the grid goes down. They're more expensive than pure grid-tied but far cheaper than full off-grid if the utility connection already exists.

If you're deciding which path fits your property, the [U.S. Department of Energy's Homeowner's Guide to Going Solar](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has a clear breakdown of system types with actual diagrams. Worth ten minutes.

## Sizing the System: Don't Guess at This

The single most common mistake in DIY off-grid solar is undersizing the battery bank while oversizing the panels. Panels are cheap and visible. Batteries are expensive and hidden. People skimp on the wrong thing.

Start with your load calculation. List every appliance you expect to run, its wattage, and the hours per day you'll use it. Multiply watts by hours to get watt-hours per day for each item, then add them up. Here's a realistic example for a modest 3-season cabin:

**Example:** Assume a cabin with a 12 cu. ft. refrigerator (60W average draw, running ~8 hours equivalent per day = 480 Wh), LED lighting at 100W for 4 hours (400 Wh), a water pump at 500W for 0.5 hours (250 Wh), device charging and miscellaneous at 200 Wh, and a small laptop at 45W for 3 hours (135 Wh). Total daily load: approximately 1,465 Wh, call it 1.5 kWh/day.

For 3 days of autonomy (meaning 3 cloudy days with no meaningful solar input), you need 4.5 kWh of usable storage. Lead-acid batteries should only be discharged to 50% to protect longevity, so you'd need 9 kWh of nameplate capacity in lead-acid. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries can go to 80–90% depth of discharge, so you'd need roughly 5–5.6 kWh nameplate. The difference in physical size and weight is dramatic, and over a 10-year lifespan, LiFePO4 usually wins on cost per cycle.

For panel sizing, divide your daily load by the peak sun hours at your location. Use NREL's PVWatts Calculator to look up your specific location. If your cabin gets 4.5 peak sun hours per day and your load is 1.5 kWh, you need at least 1,500 ÷ 4.5 = 333W of panels, plus a margin of 25–30% for inefficiency, shading, and temperature derating. So roughly 400–450W minimum. That's two standard 200W panels. Add more if you want faster battery recovery or run the system in winter.

Use a tool like the [Battery Calc](/solar-battery-calculator/) to sanity-check your bank sizing once you have your load numbers, particularly if your autonomy days or depth-of-discharge assumptions are different from the ones above.

## Permits and Insurance: The Part Everyone Skips

The idea that rural properties are permit-free zones is a myth that keeps circulating. County building departments vary, but most jurisdictions require a permit for any electrical work that includes new panel installation, a new sub-panel, or battery storage above a certain capacity. Some counties specifically exempt small off-grid systems under a threshold, say 1 kW, but many do not.

Why does this matter for a cabin? Two reasons. First, your homeowner's or vacation home insurance policy almost certainly requires that electrical systems meet local code. An unpermitted installation that causes a fire may result in a denied claim. Second, if you sell the property, an unpermitted system either has to be disclosed or removed. Neither is a good surprise for buyers.

The process is usually: call your county building department, ask what's required for a residential off-grid solar installation, and get the answer in writing if possible. Many counties have adopted the National Electrical Code (NEC), and Article 690 covers solar photovoltaic systems specifically. If you're doing the work yourself, most jurisdictions that allow owner-builder permits will let you pull one, though you'll need to pass inspection.

HOA rules are a separate matter. Some vacation communities have architectural review processes that require pre-approval for any exterior modification. Ground-mounted arrays, in particular, can run into visibility restrictions. Get written approval before you buy equipment.

## Contractor Red Flags for Cabin Solar

There are several patterns worth knowing. A contractor who quotes a flat-price system without first asking for a load analysis is guessing at your battery bank size. That's a problem. Reputable installers start with a load audit.

Watch out for quotes that don't specify battery chemistry. "Lithium" is not enough. Lithium cobalt oxide (used in phone batteries) has very different cycle life and thermal safety characteristics from LiFePO4. Any serious off-grid proposal should state the specific cell chemistry.

Another flag: no mention of charge controller type. PWM controllers are cheaper but significantly less efficient than MPPT, especially when panel voltage is higher than battery voltage. On a system with a 24V or 48V battery bank and standard 60-cell panels, the efficiency gap between PWM and MPPT is real. Any installer recommending PWM for a new build in 2026 should explain why.

The [Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)](https://www.seia.org/) maintains a directory of installers and guidance on what to expect from a professional proposal. It's a reasonable starting point for vetting contractors before you solicit bids.

Finally: get three bids and ask each contractor to show you their NABCEP certification or state electrical license number. NABCEP (North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners) is the industry credential that actually means something.

## Seasonal and Climate Realities

A 3-season cabin in New Hampshire and a year-round cabin in New Mexico are almost different problems. In northern latitudes, winter sun hours can drop below 2.5 peak hours per day, and snow loads on panels are real. Ground mounts make panel cleaning and snow clearing much easier than roof mounts.

If you heat with electricity in winter, the load calculation changes completely. A small electric resistance heater running 6 hours a day adds 6–9 kWh to your daily load. That could triple your battery and panel requirements. Propane or wood heat is almost always a better choice for a cabin that runs on solar, specifically because it removes an enormous electrical load.

For cabins that sit empty for weeks at a time, make sure your charge controller and battery management system can handle float charging without overcharging, and that your system has low-voltage disconnect to protect the battery bank if something is accidentally left on.
