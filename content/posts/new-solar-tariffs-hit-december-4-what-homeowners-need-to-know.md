The clock is running. On December 4, 2026, at 12:01 a.m., a Section 232 proclamation signed by President Trump on August 6 takes effect, setting a $0.38/W minimum import price on solar modules and $0.22/W on cells, plus a 15% tariff on polysilicon derivatives. Every panel installed after that moment gets more expensive, with no federal tax credit waiting on the other side to cushion the hit. The 30% residential solar credit under Section 25D ended for installations completed after December 31, 2025. Homeowners going solar now cannot claim the Section 25D federal tax credit, so there is no federal credit available to offset the tariff-driven cost increase.

That math is blunt. A typical fully installed residential system currently runs $20,000 to $27,000 before state incentives. Panel pricing at the project level is expected to jump from roughly $0.37/W to $0.45–$0.50/W once the tariffs land, pushing overall system costs up 3–5% according to SolarReviews. For a 10kW system at the high end of that installed-cost range, a 5% increase is $1,350 more, out of pocket, for the same hardware. Some manufacturers didn't even wait for December 4; they started raising prices in anticipation, so the creep has already started.

The last realistic contracting window is now. Permit-to-install timelines in most jurisdictions run two to six weeks, which means equipment ordered in early December won't be installed under the old pricing environment. The window that actually matters is the final ten days of November.

## What the Tariff Actually Covers

This isn't a tariff aimed at a single country. The $0.38/W minimum import price applies regardless of the panel's country of origin, including U.S.-assembled panels that use imported cells. That detail kills the common workaround of sourcing "American-made" panels from domestic assembly facilities if those facilities import their cells, which most of them do. Unbound Solar notes that the cell-level minimum of $0.22/W hits the upstream supply chain in a way that ripples through nearly every product category, including budget-tier panels that previously competed on cost.

The 15% tariff on polysilicon derivatives compounds this. Polysilicon is the base material for virtually all crystalline silicon panels, so there's no product tier that escapes the new cost structure. Premium panels from manufacturers like Maxeon or REC that were already priced above the floor will see the tariff expressed differently, but budget panels from Southeast Asian manufacturers that were priced below $0.38/W face hard price floors that eliminate their previous cost advantage entirely.

## Sizing Your System Before Prices Move

If you're trying to lock in pre-tariff pricing, don't guess at system size and don't let a contractor guess either. Pull twelve months of utility bills and find your annual kWh consumption. Divide by 1,200 for a rough kilowatt estimate (that factor accounts for average sun hours and typical panel output degradation). A 10,000 kWh/year household lands around an 8–9kW system in most of the continental U.S.

## Permits, HOA Rules, and Realistic Timelines

Permits are where good intentions meet hard calendars. Most municipalities require a building permit and an electrical permit before any residential solar installation. Many also require a utility interconnection application. Processing times range from one week in permitting-friendly jurisdictions (Arizona, Texas, Florida) to four or more weeks in slower markets. Some California counties with backlogged building departments are running six weeks right now.

Add the HOA layer and the timeline tightens further. The Solar Rights Act covers homeowners in California and similar statutes exist in roughly 25 states, limiting HOA authority to block solar installations, but HOAs can still require architectural review submissions with response windows of 30–60 days. If you live in an HOA and haven't submitted an architectural review application yet, you may already be outside a viable pre-tariff window.

The practical checklist: get your HOA application in immediately if applicable, call your local building department for a realistic permit timeline, and don't sign a contract without confirming the contractor has pulled permits for similar projects in your jurisdiction recently.

## Contractor Red Flags in a Tariff-Rush Environment

Urgency creates pressure, and pressure is a contractor's best friend. When tariffs, expiring credits, or policy changes push homeowners to act fast, the quality of contracting decisions drops. Watch for these specific problems.

A "lock in pricing today" pitch without a written equipment specification is worthless. Any contract worth signing names the exact panel model, cell type, wattage rating, inverter make and model, and racking system. Vague references to "tier-1 panels" or "premium equipment" aren't binding. Insist on a line-item equipment schedule as part of the contract before you sign anything.

Watch for contractors pushing systems substantially larger than your load analysis supports. A contractor quoting a 14kW system to a household consuming 9,000 kWh annually is either miscalculating or overselling. That gap at current pricing represents roughly $8,000 in unnecessary hardware, plus a larger post-tariff price increase.

Verify licensing and insurance before any deposit changes hands. Solar installer licensing requirements vary by state, but every state requires electrical contractor licensing. Check your state contractor licensing board directly, not the contractor's website. And get at least three bids. A rushed market doesn't change that math.

## State Incentives That Still Apply

The federal Section 25D credit is no longer available for systems installed after December 31, 2025. State-level programs remain available, increasing the overall incentive picture. Massachusetts offers a 15% state income tax credit capped at $1,000. New York's residential solar credit covers 25% of installed costs up to $5,000. Maryland's Clean Energy Grant provides up to $1,000 per system. California's SGIP battery storage incentive is separate from panel credits but relevant if you're pairing storage with your array.

Net metering policies are equally important and vary significantly. Some utilities pay retail rates for exported solar; others pay avoided-cost rates, which can be as low as 3–4 cents per kWh. That difference dramatically changes payback period calculations. Before you finalize system size, get your utility's current interconnection and net metering tariff in writing, not from the contractor's sales sheet but from the utility directly.

The tariff takes effect in days, not weeks. If you're seriously considering solar, the time to get bids, check permits, and review contracts is now, not after December 4 when the price floor is already set.
